How this system scores candidates

Every number the ranking engine produces, in plain language — what it measures, how much it counts, what is automatically verified, and the fifteen judgment calls that need a human owner before these scores are used in a hiring decision.

What this document is for

The system reads résumés, compares them against a job description, and produces a ranked shortlist. Each candidate gets a single headline score between 0 and 1, built from smaller scores underneath it.

None of those numbers are objective facts. They are the arithmetic consequence of choices someone made — that skills matter more than education, that a missing must-have halves a score rather than disqualifying, that a degree outside the fields a job named earns only partial education credit. Those are hiring policy, written as decimals. This document exposes each one so you can ratify it, change it, or reject it.

Where you see Needs ratification, the question is not "is this working?" It is "is this what we intend?"

The Recruiter

role: recruiter

Does the operational work: creates the job, uploads résumés, generates the shortlist, and is one of only two roles permitted to un-blind a candidate's identity. In practice a Recruiter can do everything an Admin can — the two roles are not distinguished by any route in the system today.

The Hiring Manager

role: hiring_manager

Read-only, and blind. Sees the ranked shortlist with names, emails, phone numbers and identifying filenames removed. Cannot upload, cannot re-run a shortlist, and cannot reveal a candidate's identity — that request has to go through a Recruiter, which is what makes the reveal auditable.

The Auditor

role: auditor

Currently identical to Hiring Manager: the same blind reads, nothing more. The role exists so that permissions are ready, but Known gap there is no screen for reading the reveal audit log. Retrieving it today means asking an engineer to run a database query.

The Admin

role: admin

Unrestricted. Also the role everyone silently becomes if authentication is switched off — the system logs a loud warning at startup when it is, and refuses to start on several misconfigurations that would quietly collapse two roles into one.

Known gap  Permissions are not scoped to a person's own requisitions

There is no concept of "my jobs." Any Hiring Manager key grants read access to every job, every résumé and every shortlist in the company. If you intend a manager to see only their own openings, that control does not exist today and cannot be configured — it needs building.

The full pipeline. Consent is a hard gate at the very front — refuse it and no candidate data is stored at all.

Stage 01  Building the pool

The system finds up to 50 résumés whose overall content is closest to the job description, using a mathematical similarity comparison rather than keyword matching. Only résumés uploaded for that specific job are eligible — nothing is pulled from other requisitions.

Stage 02  Structured scoring

Every candidate in the pool is scored on five measurable dimensions — skills, experience, education, seniority and overall similarity. This is the arithmetic layer, and it is fully deterministic: the same résumé against the same job always produces the same number.

Stage 03  Evidence gathering

For the top 15 candidates only, a language model goes back into the résumé and pulls out the actual sentences that prove each requirement is met. Every quote it produces is then verified against the real document — see how quotes are checked.

Stage 04  Final ranking

The structured score, the evidence score and the motivation score are combined into the headline number, and candidates are ordered by it.

Two layers of weighting. The five structured dimensions combine into one score, which is then worth 60% of the headline number.

The three top-level components

Structured score0.60

The measurable, arithmetic assessment: skills, experience, education, seniority, similarity. Deterministic and repeatable.

Evidence completeness0.30

What fraction of the job's requirements were proven with a verified quote from the résumé. A fully met requirement counts 1, a partial counts 0.5, unproven counts 0.

Motivation0.10

Read from the cover letter only. Themes the model claims to find are only counted if they clear a confidence bar and can be traced to a real sentence — but the average is taken across all themes it proposed, so unverifiable enthusiasm actively drags the score down. A candidate with no cover letter scores 0 here.

Inside the structured score

Skills0.40

Each required skill is scored on years of use, how recently it was used, and how directly it matches. Recency is banded: used within 2 years scores full marks, within 5 years scores 0.7, older scores 0.4. A related-but-not-exact skill earns half credit.

Missing a must-have halves the entire skills score — it does not disqualify. A strongly-matched candidate missing one mandatory skill can still outrank a weaker candidate who has it.

Experience0.25

Measured against the job's stated minimum. Meeting it scores full marks. Substantially exceeding it is dampened — a candidate with more than double the required experience starts losing points, though never below 0.8. This is deliberate over-qualification handling.

Seniority0.15

Compares the candidate's most recent job title against the job's title. A candidate with no detectable recent title scores 0.

Education0.10

Reads the level — high school through PhD. Meeting or exceeding the required level scores full marks; a lower level earns partial credit; no degree scores 0.

Field of study now counts when the job names specific fields: a candidate who meets the level but whose qualifying degree is in a field the job did not name is capped at partial credit. The match is fuzzy, so a minor spelling or format variation of a named field still counts. A job that names no fields still scores on level alone, exactly as before. A degree whose field could not be read from the résumé is treated as a non-match — see register item 3 for that trade-off. (ADR-028)

Overall similarity0.10

How closely the résumé as a whole resembles the job description. This score is relative to the other candidates in the pool, not absolute — the strongest résumé in any given batch scores 1.0 here regardless of how good it actually is.

How quotes are verified

The safeguard against a language model inventing a qualification.

The main risk in using a language model for hiring is fabrication: the model asserting a candidate has an accreditation they never claimed. Three independent checks run against that, and the last is the strongest — every quote shown to a human must survive a match against the real document.

flowchart TD
  Q["Model produces a quote
+ says which passage it came from"] --> C1{"Does that passage
actually exist?"}
  C1 -->|No| DROP["Citation discarded"]
  C1 -->|Yes| C2{"Does the quote match
the real text ≥ 85%?"}
  C2 -->|No| BLANK["Quote deleted.
'Met' downgraded to 'missing'.
Confidence capped at 0.3"]
  C2 -->|Yes| KEEP["Quote shown to reviewer,
counts toward the score"]
  DROP --> C2

Evidence verification. A quote that cannot be traced back to the candidate's own words is removed before any human sees it.

This threshold was not guessed. It was chosen by measuring four deliberately fabricated quotes against four genuine ones and picking the comparison method that rejected all four fakes while accepting all four real quotes. A looser method that was tested let a fabrication through at 0.855.

Test-gated  This one you can rely on

Every release must show a 100% verification rate — every quote surfaced traces to real text — and must correctly reject all four known fabrications. If either fails, the change cannot ship.

One caveat on "automatic"

These run against a fixed set of 20 test résumés, and the routine version uses a stand-in for the language model rather than calling the real one. The full live measurement against the actual model is a manual step a human performs before merging — not something the machine enforces on its own.

Privacy, consent and the audit trail

Consent is a hard gate

The upload form requires an explicit tick: "I confirm the candidate consented to this processing (PIPEDA/FIPPA)." Without it the file is never sent, never stored, and no processing occurs. The confirmation is stored permanently against the upload.

Needs ratification  Consent is recorded as a single yes/no per upload batch

There is no timestamp of its own, no record of which consent wording the candidate agreed to, and no per-candidate granularity within a batch — one tick covers every file in that upload. If your retention or subject-access obligations require per-candidate, per-version consent records, this does not currently meet that bar.

How identity is protected

  • Names, emails, phone numbers and cover letter text are encrypted in the database. The application only ever handles encrypted bytes.
  • Email addresses are additionally stored as a one-way hash, deliberately unencrypted — this is what lets you locate a candidate by email in response to a subject-access request without unlocking anything.
  • Identity is stripped before any text reaches the ranking maths, including out of the numeric vectors. The system treats those vectors as personal data in their own right.
  • Under blind review, the Hiring Manager's view removes names, emails, phones and identifying filenames — including from quoted passages, so a résumé header cannot leak a name through an evidence quote.

The reveal sequence. The audit row is written before anything is decrypted — so an identity cannot be viewed without leaving a record.

The ordering here is deliberate and worth understanding: permission is checked before the candidate's existence is probed, and the audit entry is written before decryption. A rejected attempt cannot create a misleading audit row, and a successful one cannot avoid creating a real one.

Two bulk reveal routes were removed during this work — including one that de-anonymised an entire shortlist in a single unaudited download.

Known gap  The audit log cannot name the person who revealed

Every reveal triggered through the web interface is recorded with the actor as api — not the individual who clicked. The browser interface shares one system credential for all users, and the name field it could send is not filled in and would not be verified if it were.

What this means practically: you can prove that a candidate's identity was revealed, when, for which job, and from which screen. You cannot prove who did it. If your policy requires individual accountability for un-blinding, this needs building before these logs can support it.

Known gap  Turning blind review off is not audited at all

Blind review is a per-job setting. A Recruiter or Admin can switch it off, which permanently un-blinds every candidate on that job for every future viewer — and unlike a single reveal, this action writes no audit record whatsoever.

The internal review describes this as a wider blast radius than the reveal endpoint that an entire audit table was built for. It is the single item on this page most worth fixing.

The ratification register

Fifteen decisions currently encoded in the software. Each one needs an owner who confirms it reflects our hiring policy.

1 Skills are worth 40% of the structured score; education 10%. A defensible weighting for technical roles. May be indefensible for credential-regulated ones. Ratify
2 Missing a mandatory skill halves the skills score rather than excluding the candidate. Determines whether "must-have" is genuinely a requirement or merely a strong preference. Ratify
3 Field of study caps education at partial credit when a qualifying-level degree is in a field the job did not name; a degree whose field cannot be read is treated as non-matching; the field match is fuzzy at a 0.85 similarity bar. Resolves the former "field ignored" gap (ADR-028), but the cap size, the fuzzy bar, and penalising an unreadable field are hiring-policy choices — the last can under-credit a genuinely qualified candidate whose résumé parse dropped the field. All three are configurable without a code change. Ratify
4 Nice-to-have skills contribute nothing to the structured score. They earn credit only through evidence. Stating them may mislead candidates about their weight. Gap
5 Only the top 15 candidates receive evidence review. Candidate 16 scores zero on 40% of the headline number by structure, not by merit. A hard cliff. Ratify
6 A skill with no stated duration is treated as fully meeting the requirement. A generous default. Résumés rarely state per-skill years, so this fires often. Ratify
7 Substantially over-qualified candidates are scored down, with a floor. Deliberate dampening. Carries obvious age-proxy risk and should be a conscious, defended choice. Ratify
8 Skills used within 2 years score full marks; 5 years 0.7; older 0.4. Penalises career breaks, parental leave and caring responsibilities. Human-rights exposure. Ratify
9 Overall similarity is scored relative to the batch, not absolutely. The best of a weak pool scores full marks. Scores are not comparable between requisitions. Ratify
10 A cover letter is worth 10%; candidates without one score zero there. A structural penalty for not submitting an optional document. Ratify
11 If the language model fails during ranking — either it times out/errors, or it returns malformed or empty output — the shortlist is withheld rather than shown with a silently-zeroed candidate. The job displays "Waiting for AI to rank candidates…" and retries automatically, up to a configurable ceiling, before it stops and stays visibly in that state for a human to notice. Closes the former gap (ADR-029): a technical failure can no longer look identical to a genuinely weak candidate. What is left to own is the policy: how many times to retry and how long to wait between attempts before giving up and asking a human to re-run it. Ratify
12 Any Hiring Manager or Auditor can read every job in the company. No per-requisition scoping exists. Likely exceeds intended need-to-know. Gap
13 Reveals from the web interface are attributed to api, not a named person. Individual accountability for un-blinding cannot currently be demonstrated. Gap
14 Switching off blind review writes no audit record. The highest-impact privacy action in the system is the least observable. Gap
15 Consent is one yes/no per upload batch, with no wording version or timestamp. May not satisfy per-candidate consent evidence obligations. Ratify

Two operational realities worth knowing

Scores from résumé-to-job matching are not comparable to job-to-résumé matching. The reverse direction omits the motivation component entirely, so its scores top out at 0.9 rather than 1.0. Never place the two side by side. Ranking quality in that direction is also not yet covered by the automatic checks.

A résumé can silently fail to process. If document processing fails, the résumé stays marked "uploaded" indefinitely rather than showing an error — the same state it would show if it had only just arrived. A résumé stuck in that state is invisible to the shortlist and the candidate is simply never ranked. This has happened in practice: on 19 July 2026 a batch of 16 sat unprocessed for roughly eighteen hours, showing no error at any point. Until it is fixed, if a batch of candidates seems to have vanished from a shortlist, check that they finished processing before concluding they scored poorly.

A résumé can also process only partially — this is now flagged, not silent (ADR-030). In that same batch, 10 of the 16 finished with their skills extraction having failed — skills fell back to a basic keyword scan. That gap used to be invisible outside the server logs; it no longer is. A résumé in that state is marked degraded, counted separately in the per-job status breakdown ("N parsed (M degraded)"), and badged on both the résumé list and its detail page with the reason. It is also excluded from shortlists and reverse-match results until it is re-parsed, rather than ranked on the incomplete skill data — today that means re-uploading the résumé; a dedicated re-parse action is a documented follow-up, not yet built.

Suggested next steps

  1. Assign an owner to each of the fifteen register items. The eleven marked "Ratify" need a documented yes or a changed number; all of them are configurable without code changes.
  2. Treat items 13 and 14 as blocking if these scores will inform real hiring decisions — unattributable reveals and unaudited un-blinding are the two that would be hardest to defend if challenged.
  3. Decide whether items 7 and 8 survive legal review before first production use. Over-qualification dampening and skill-recency banding are the two most likely to produce adverse impact.
